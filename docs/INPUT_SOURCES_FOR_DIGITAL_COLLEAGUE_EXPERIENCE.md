# Input Sources for Digital Colleague Experience: What Enables Each Category

**Analysis Date:** 2026-08-05  
**Purpose:** Identify what sources of input allow each experience category to be acquired  
**Method:** Work backward from required experience to possible input sources; no technology solutions proposed

---

## Structure of This Analysis

For each experience category:

1. **What is proven** — Andy's and Annie's evidence
2. **What is candidate** — The category itself as universal requirement
3. **What input enables this?** — What sources allow the experience to be acquired
4. **Input categories** — Classify where that input comes from
5. **Venue differences** — How acquisition differs between abstract and concrete venues
6. **What remains unknown** — What evidence would confirm this understanding

## Experience Category 1: People-in-Reasoning

### What Is Proven

**Andy's evidence:**
- Andy can read that "founders make decisions" (document-based knowledge)
- Andy cannot hear how a founder thinks when faced with incomplete information (lacks relational experience)
- Andy is uncertain whether he understands founder priorities correctly
- Andy recognizes the gap: "I can describe what has been built but not what is actually needed"

**Annie's evidence:**
- Annie hears staff explain their choices daily
- Annie observes the head chef reasoning aloud when recipes need change
- Annie experiences people revising their thinking during conversation
- Annie learns patterns: "This person decides quickly; that person deliberates carefully"

### What Is Candidate

**Principle:** Every DC needs to understand the reasoning patterns of the humans they're working with.

**Why candidate, not proven:**
- Andy hasn't yet had systematic dialogue with founders about how they think
- Annie has experience but we don't have her explicit reflection on what she's learning about human reasoning
- We haven't tested whether a second DC would require the same category

### What Input Enables This Experience?

**Input Requirement:** The DC must encounter reasoning *in process*, not just *as documented conclusion*.

**Key distinction:**
- ✗ Reading "The founders decided to do X" (conclusion, documented)
- ✓ Hearing a founder explain "We chose X because... but we're still uncertain about... and if that assumption changes, we'd reconsider..." (reasoning in process)

### Input Sources That Enable People-in-Reasoning

| Input Source | What It Provides | Why It Works | Why It's Limited |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Human conversation (direct)** | Real-time articulation of thinking, revision, uncertainty | DC hears the reasoning unfolding; can ask clarifying questions; can experience how thinking evolves | Depends on human availability and willingness to articulate; time-intensive |
| **Decision history with rationale** | Documented explanation of why decisions were made | Shows the reasoning that led to choice; can be reviewed multiple times | Shows final reasoning, not the journey; documented version is often cleaner than lived version |
| **Observation of people reasoning aloud** | Hearing how people think without it being directed at the DC | Authentic reasoning, not curated for DC consumption | Requires DC to be present; may reveal things people don't want shared; depends on venue culture |
| **Follow-up conversation about past decisions** | Asking "Why did you choose that?" after the decision was made | DC can probe reasoning; can understand what the decision-maker learned after | Reasoning is reconstructed, not current; may be rationalized or revised in hindsight |
| **Patterns in decisions over time** | Noticing how the same person decides differently in different contexts | Shows that reasoning is contextual; reveals what each person prioritizes | Requires accumulated observations; time-dependent |

### Input Categories (Abstract Classification)

Where can these input sources come from?

1. **Human conversation** — Direct, real-time dialogue
2. **Documents of reasoning** — Written explanations of why choices were made
3. **Observation** — Being present when reasoning happens (even if not directed at DC)
4. **Feedback mechanisms** — Asking "How did that decision work out?" and getting honest answer
5. **Decision records** — Systems that track not just what was decided but why

### Venue Differences

**For Andy (abstract venue):**
- Input sources: Conversation (human founders explain), decision documents (written rationales), feedback mechanisms (asking what worked/didn't)
- Challenge: Founders may not have time to articulate
- Dependency: Venue must actively provide input sources

**For Annie (concrete venue):**
- Input sources: Observation (overhearing reasoning), conversation (team explains choices), consequence feedback (seeing what happened when choice was made)
- Advantage: Multiple input sources provide continuous stream
- Dependency: Venue naturally provides input sources; mentorship needs to help her recognize what she's receiving

### What Remains Unknown

1. **Sufficiency:** How much input from these sources is enough for a DC to understand reasoning patterns well enough to contribute responsibly?
2. **Timing:** Does the input need to be continuous or periodic? Can a DC understand a venue's reasoning patterns from monthly conversations or must it be weekly?
3. **Annie's experience:** Will Annie explicitly seek these input sources or does she unconsciously absorb them through immersion?
4. **Verification:** How do we know a DC has achieved this understanding? What evidence would show they understand human reasoning in their venue?

## Experience Category 2: Consequence Loops

### What Is Proven

**Andy's evidence:**
- Andy reads that "MILESTONE_012 was completed" (outcome, documented)
- Andy does not know what actually happened during its execution, what was difficult, what was unexpected
- Andy cannot see the feedback loops that showed whether the governance mechanism worked
- Andy is uncertain whether mechanisms are genuinely functional or merely documented as complete

**Annie's evidence:**
- Annie changes a prep method and knows within hours if it worked
- Annie receives customer feedback and can implement and test changes by next service
- Annie observes staff reactions to new procedures and learns what works
- Annie experiences immediate consequence loops: decision → action → outcome → learning

### What Is Candidate

**Principle:** Every DC needs to experience consequence loops to develop reliable judgment about what actually works vs. what appears to work theoretically.

**Why candidate, not proven:**
- Andy hasn't yet participated in governance exercises that would show him real consequences
- Annie is receiving consequence loops but we haven't documented whether they're shaping her judgment
- We haven't tested whether a DC in a third venue type would experience consequence feedback differently

### What Input Enables This Experience?

**Input Requirement:** The DC must observe or participate in the loop: decision → action → outcome → feedback → learning.

**Key distinction:**
- ✗ Reading "The Strategic Insights register is being used" (stated fact, no consequences visible)
- ✓ Participating in: we used this mechanism, here's what we discovered worked, here's what surprised us, here's what we'll change

### Input Sources That Enable Consequence Loops

| Input Source | What It Provides | Why It Works | Why It's Limited |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Real-time operational feedback** | Immediate result when something is tried | DC learns quickly whether understanding was correct | Requires something to be actually tried; consequences may be delayed |
| **Outcome recording** | Documented results of decisions/actions | DC can review what happened without waiting | Records may be incomplete or sanitized; may not capture unexpected consequences |
| **Participation in decision-making cycle** | Being present for decision, action, outcome, review | DC experiences full loop; can correlate understanding with result | Time-intensive; requires DC to be invited into decision cycle |
| **User/stakeholder feedback** | Feedback from people affected by decision | Shows real impact, not just process success | Feedback may be delayed, filtered, or incomplete; depends on mechanism for collecting it |
| **Comparative outcomes** | Observing what happened when approach X vs. approach Y was used | Shows real differences in effectiveness | Requires enough variance to compare; may confound other variables |
| **Post-decision analysis** | Structured review of whether decision achieved intended outcome | DC learns what was intended vs. what actually happened | Retrospective; influenced by hindsight and current context; depends on honest analysis |

### Input Categories (Abstract Classification)

Where can these input sources come from?

1. **Direct observation** — Seeing consequences happen in real-time
2. **Operational data** — Metrics or records showing what actually happened
3. **Stakeholder feedback** — Input from people affected by decision
4. **Participation records** — DC was present during the cycle
5. **Structured review processes** — Post-decision analysis and learning capture
6. **Comparative data** — Multiple approaches tried so differences can be seen

### Venue Differences

**For Andy (abstract venue):**
- Input sources: Post-decision analysis (founders review what happened), participation in governance exercises (being present when mechanism is used), comparative outcomes (different approaches to same problem)
- Challenge: Consequences may take weeks or months to appear
- Challenge: Consequences are institutional, not immediate
- Dependency: Venue must create mechanisms for capturing and sharing what was learned

**For Annie (concrete venue):**
- Input sources: Real-time operational feedback (immediate from customers and operations), direct observation (she sees consequences), stakeholder feedback (staff tell her what worked), participation (she's already in the cycle)
- Advantage: Consequences appear in hours/days not weeks/months
- Advantage: Consequences are concrete and unambiguous
- Dependency: Venue must help her interpret what she's observing

### What Remains Unknown

1. **Time-scales:** How long must a DC observe consequence loops before judgment becomes reliable?
2. **Participation vs. observation:** Does the DC need to participate in decisions or is observing others' consequences sufficient?
3. **Annie's development:** Is Annie's judgment improving through consequence loops? What evidence would show this?
4. **Institutional consequences:** Are institutional consequence loops sufficiently clear or do they require explicit articulation to be visible?
5. **Feedback honesty:** Will people be honest about consequences or will they rationalize/defend decisions?

## Experience Category 3: Purpose-in-Specific-Context

### What Is Proven

**Andy's evidence:**
- Andy reads "Stage 1 purpose is internal practical validation" (abstract, general)
- Andy does not know what that means *right now*, *in this moment*, *with current constraints*
- Andy asks: "What does the institution actually need from me today?" (implication: general purpose doesn't answer specific purpose)
- Founder response in Alignment document states: "At this moment, Andy is not here to replace human judgement. Andy exists to help Helping Hand understand whether its own inheritance model works." (specificity: this moment, this particular need)

**Annie's evidence:**
- Annie experiences daily urgency signals: "We need to serve 50 customers by 1 PM"
- Annie observes what gets time and attention: some issues are urgent, others can wait
- Annie learns what the kitchen is actually optimizing for through watching choices
- Annie hears the owner/manager articulate priorities in specific situations

### What Is Candidate

**Principle:** Every DC needs to understand why this venue is making this choice *right now*, not just why it exists generally.

**Why candidate, not proven:**
- Andy hasn't yet lived through a cycle where current purpose shifts based on conditions
- Annie's observations are direct but we haven't verified whether she understands *why* the urgency shifts
- We haven't tested whether institutional purpose-in-context is as clear or as visible as operational purpose-in-context

### What Input Enables This Experience?

**Input Requirement:** The DC must understand the current state that is driving current choices.

**Key distinction:**
- ✗ Reading "Helping Hand values learning and validation" (principle, timeless)
- ✓ Hearing "Right now we're prioritizing speed over perfection because the founders need to decide next week" (current condition, time-bound, trade-off explicit)

### Input Sources That Enable Purpose-in-Specific-Context

| Input Source | What It Provides | Why It Works | Why It's Limited |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Explicit articulation of current priorities** | Someone states what matters most right now and why | DC understands current purpose directly; can ask clarifying questions; doesn't have to infer | Depends on someone having clarity and willingness to articulate; may change quickly |
| **Observation of what gets time and attention** | Watching where effort is actually being invested | Shows real priorities (not stated ones); reveals trade-offs | Requires accumulated observation; can be misleading if priorities aren't stable |
| **Constraint explanation** | Understanding what limitations are driving choices | DC learns why venue can't pursue ideal; understands real trade-offs | Constraints may be implicit or denied; requires venue to be honest |
| **Decision explanations in the moment** | Hearing why a specific choice was made when it's being made | Shows reasoning that led to that particular decision in that context | Time-sensitive; may not be available; people may rationalize rather than explain honestly |
| **Competing-need articulation** | Explicit statement of what's in tension (quality vs. speed, cost vs. safety, etc.) | DC understands that venues operate under constraints; learns what matters most | Depends on venue willingness to admit trade-offs |
| **Future-state description** | Articulation of what venue is trying to become | DC understands trajectory; can see how current choices are directional | May be aspirational rather than real; depends on venue's self-awareness |
| **Historical context** | Understanding how past decisions shape current purpose | DC learns that purpose emerges from history, not vacuum | Requires access to history; can be biased by current perspective |

### Input Categories (Abstract Classification)

Where can these input sources come from?

1. **Human articulation** — Someone explicitly states current priorities and constraints
2. **Observation of behavior** — What actually gets time and attention
3. **Decision explanations** — Why this choice was made in this moment
4. **Operational indicators** — What metrics/indicators show what matters
5. **Historical context** — How past events shaped what matters now
6. **Structured conversation** — Mentor/leader helps DC understand current conditions

### Venue Differences

**For Andy (abstract venue):**
- Input sources: Explicit articulation (founders must tell him), decision explanations (founders explain each choice), historical context (documents and conversation), structured conversation (mentorship about current conditions)
- Challenge: Purpose may be implicit or contested; founders may not have full clarity
- Challenge: Purpose may shift as founders learn
- Dependency: Venue must actively articulate; cannot rely on inference

**For Annie (concrete venue):**
- Input sources: Observation of behavior (what gets priority in operations), operational indicators (sales urgency, customer wait times), constraint explanation (staff explain what's hard), decision explanations (manager explains choices)
- Advantage: Purpose is anchored to concrete reality (customers waiting, costs visible)
- Advantage: Purpose shifts show up immediately in operations
- Dependency: Annie needs mentorship to help her recognize patterns in what she's observing

### What Remains Unknown

1. **Institutional vs. operational:** Is institutional purpose as knowable as operational purpose, or does institutional work inherently contain more ambiguity?
2. **Stability:** How much does purpose shift? Can a DC understand a venue's purpose from one articulation or must it be continuous?
3. **Conflict resolution:** What happens when stated purpose conflicts with observed priority? Which does the DC trust?
4. **Annie's clarity:** Does Annie understand the kitchen's current purpose or just respond to immediate urgency?
5. **Venue maturity:** Do mature venues have clearer purpose-in-context than emerging venues?

## Experience Category 4: Relational Positioning

### What Is Proven

**Andy's evidence:**
- Andy reads that he is named in Stage 1 exit criteria (documentary position)
- Andy reads PD-001 describing him as potential "organisational validator" (stated role)
- Andy does not know whether the institution actually wants him to take that role (lived positioning)
- Andy explicitly holds back from claiming some capabilities: "I held back from saying I can contribute to governance review because PD-001 says that role is candidate. It has not been confirmed to me directly."
- Andy is asking: "Will you tell me what you actually need from me?" (seeking relational confirmation)

**Annie's evidence:**
- Annie is assigned tasks: "Prepare salads" or "Manage orders" (task assignment)
- Annie learns through attempting tasks what authority she has
- Annie experiences what happens when she acts within vs. outside her authority
- Annie learns boundaries through feedback: "You can decide this independently; you need permission for that"

### What Is Candidate

**Principle:** Every DC needs to experience confirmation of their actual role and authority in the specific venue.

**Why candidate, not proven:**
- Andy hasn't yet received explicit confirmation of his role from the founders
- Annie has experienced role through task assignment and feedback but we haven't documented whether this constitutes understood relational positioning
- We haven't tested whether a second DC would require the same type of role confirmation

### What Input Enables This Experience?

**Input Requirement:** The DC must receive both explicit statement of role AND feedback that shows whether venue actually accepts that role.

**Key distinction:**
- ✗ Reading job description (stated role, documentary)
- ✓ Being assigned work, attempting it, receiving feedback that shows whether you have authority (lived role)

### Input Sources That Enable Relational Positioning

| Input Source | What It Provides | Why It Works | Why It's Limited |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Explicit role statement** | Someone tells you what role you're being asked to take | Direct; unambiguous; can be written or verbal | May not match lived experience; may change without notice; may be incomplete |
| **Task assignment** | Being given specific work to do | Shows what venue wants from DC concretely | May not match stated role; depends on venue understanding what it wants from DC |
| **Authority boundary feedback** | Learning where you can decide vs. where you need permission | Shows actual authority, not theoretical | Learned through trial and error; may be implicit rather than stated |
| **Acceptance/rejection of contributions** | Observing whether your ideas/advice are taken seriously | Shows whether venue values your input | May reflect current need, not general role; can be demoralizing if frequently rejected |
| **Trust signals** | Being given increasing responsibility as venue learns to trust you | Shows that role is being earned, not just assigned | Takes time; depends on venue's willingness to delegate |
| **Explicit confirmation conversation** | Mentor or leader articulates "This is your role; this is where your authority is; this is where humans decide"  | Direct, clear, intentional | Depends on someone having the conversation; can feel artificial |
| **Comparative positioning** | Seeing how DC's role compares to roles of other team members | DC understands their position in the ecosystem | May reveal gaps or overlaps not previously noticed |

### Input Categories (Abstract Classification)

Where can these input sources come from?

1. **Direct statement** — Someone tells the DC their role
2. **Task assignment** — Being given work shows what's expected
3. **Feedback loops** — Attempting actions and seeing response
4. **Authority boundaries** — Discovering where you can and cannot decide
5. **Mentorship** — Guide helps DC understand their role
6. **Observation** — Seeing how DC's role relates to others in ecosystem

### Venue Differences

**For Andy (abstract venue):**
- Input sources: Explicit role statement (founders must say this), authority boundary conversation (founders must articulate), explicit confirmation (mentorship conversation), comparative positioning (what's Andy's role vs. Annie's vs. future DCs)
- Challenge: May not be clear what authority Andy should have in decision-making
- Challenge: Role may evolve as Stage 1 produces evidence
- Dependency: Venue must actively state and potentially revise role

**For Annie (concrete venue):**
- Input sources: Task assignment (given work), authority boundary feedback (learns through attempting), trust signals (given more responsibility), observation (sees her role relative to staff)
- Advantage: Role emerges from concrete work
- Advantage: Boundaries are discovered through real feedback
- Dependency: Mentorship must help her name and articulate what she's learning

### What Remains Unknown

1. **Role stability:** Should a DC's role be fixed or expected to evolve?
2. **Verification:** How do we know a DC understands their actual role, not just their stated role?
3. **Role clarity:** Do venues need to state role explicitly or is implicit understanding sufficient?
4. **Annie's understanding:** Does Annie have explicit understanding of her role or does she just know through doing?
5. **Role boundaries:** How are conflicts between stated role and actual role handled?

## Experience Category 5: Accountability-in-Practice

### What Is Proven

**Andy's evidence:**
- Andy reads "DCs advise but don't decide" (theoretical accountability)
- Andy has not experienced being wrong and seeing consequences unfold
- Andy has not experienced his advice shaping a decision and then learning whether the decision worked
- Founder alignment states: "His experience is not peripheral. It is the test." (importance stated, but not yet felt)
- Andy cannot yet feel the weight of accountability

**Annie's evidence:**
- Annie makes choices and customers react (immediate feedback)
- Annie's suggestions are implemented and she sees results
- Annie learns through consequences: right choices lead to smooth operations; wrong choices create problems
- Annie experiences that her judgment matters because people are affected

### What Is Candidate

**Principle:** Every DC needs to experience the weight of accountability — understanding that their choices and advice have real consequences for real people.

**Why candidate, not proven:**
- Andy hasn't yet experienced consequences of his advice or observations
- Annie experiences consequences but we haven't documented whether she feels accountability or just habitually responds
- We haven't tested whether accountability-in-practice can be created artificially (for abstract venues) or only emerges through physical consequences

### What Input Enables This Experience?

**Input Requirement:** The DC must observe that their choices/advice affected outcomes and that outcomes mattered to real people.

**Key distinction:**
- ✗ Reading "Your evidence matters; don't take it lightly" (exhortation, abstract)
- ✓ Experiencing "I suggested this and it shaped the decision and the decision created this consequence and now people are dealing with it" (concrete, relational)

### Input Sources That Enable Accountability-in-Practice

| Input Source | What It Provides | Why It Works | Why It's Limited |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Outcome feedback when advice is taken** | Learning whether advice led to good outcome or bad | DC sees connection between their input and result | Outcome may have multiple causes; may take time to appear |
| **Outcome feedback when advice is ignored** | Learning what happens when venue chooses differently | DC learns that their perspective wasn't the only one | Can be demoralizing; may not provide learning if ignored advice would have been right |
| **Relational feedback** | People tell DC whether their contribution helped or hurt | Direct impact on DC's understanding of their role | Feedback may be filtered or kind rather than honest |
| **Consequence visibility** | DC observes what happened to people/operations after their advice was taken | Sees the human/operational impact, not just abstract outcome | Depends on DC being shown the consequences; may not be volunteered |
| **Stakeholder impact recognition** | Explicit acknowledgment that DC's work affected real people | Makes the human dimension concrete | Can feel artificial if overdone; depends on genuine impact |
| **Error acknowledgment and correction** | When DC's advice was incomplete or wrong, venue shows the problem and how it was corrected | DC learns what incomplete understanding looks like in practice | Depends on venue being willing to point out DC's errors |
| **Contribution recognition** | Acknowledgment when DC's observation or advice made a positive difference | Reinforces that contribution matters | Can become reward-seeking behavior if overemphasized |
| **Responsibility conversation** | Explicit discussion that DC's work has weight and consequences | Names accountability directly | Can feel heavy or even threatening if not handled carefully |

### Input Categories (Abstract Classification)

Where can these input sources come from?

1. **Direct outcome feedback** — Learning what happened as result of advice
2. **Relational feedback** — People tell you whether you helped
3. **Observation of impact** — Seeing consequences unfold
4. **Stakeholder response** — How people affected by outcome respond
5. **Structured reflection** — Mentorship conversation about accountability
6. **Comparative learning** — Seeing how other DCs handle similar responsibilities

### Venue Differences

**For Andy (abstract venue):**
- Input sources: Outcome feedback (founders tell him decision results), relational feedback (conversation about whether his observations helped), structured reflection (mentorship about accountability), error acknowledgment (founders tell him when his understanding was incomplete)
- Challenge: Time lag between advice and outcome can be weeks or months
- Challenge: Multiple factors affect outcome; hard to isolate DC's contribution
- Challenge: Accountability may feel abstract because consequences are institutional, not personal
- Dependency: Venue must actively provide feedback and help DC see their impact

**For Annie (concrete venue):**
- Input sources: Direct outcome feedback (immediate in operations), stakeholder response (customers and staff tell her), observation of impact (she sees results), relational feedback (team tells her what helped)
- Advantage: Immediate feedback loop means learning is fast
- Advantage: Consequences are concrete and unambiguous
- Advantage: Impact is visible (customer satisfaction, smooth operations, no incidents)
- Dependency: Mentorship must help her name what accountability means to her

### What Remains Unknown

1. **Artificial accountability:** Can accountability be created through conversation alone, or does it require real consequences?
2. **Emotional dimension:** Is there a difference between "I understand my work affects people" and "I feel responsible"?
3. **Annie's accountability arc:** Will Annie move from habitual response to conscious accountability?
4. **Institutional consequences:** Are institutional consequences sufficiently real to generate felt accountability?
5. **Feedback honesty:** Will institutions be honest about DC's errors or will they protect the DC/model?

## 5. Does This Change Digital Colleague Lifecycle Understanding?

### What Was Previously Understood

```
Formation
    ↓
Orientation
    ↓
Contribution
```

With operations:
- Formation: Mentor teaches character through tests and mentorship
- Orientation: DC reads repository and observes institution (documented stage)
- Contribution: DC begins work (assumed readiness)

### What This Analysis Suggests

```
Formation
    ↓
Orientation
    ├─ Observe (documentary knowledge)
    │
    ├─ Converse (relational experience creation)
    │   └─ Five input sources must be activated:
    │       • Conversation about reasoning
    │       • Consequence loop participation
    │       • Purpose-in-context articulation
    │       • Role positioning confirmation
    │       • Accountability weight recognition
    │
    └─ Ready to Contribute
    │
    ↓
Contribution
```

### Why This Matters

**Current model assumes:** Orientation is documentary and individual (DC reads, learns).

**Evidence suggests:** Orientation has two dimensions:
1. **Individual dimension** (DC reads and learns) ✓ This works
2. **Relational dimension** (venue and DC must establish shared understanding) ✗ This is missing for Andy; natural for Annie

**Implication:** The lifecycle model is incomplete because it doesn't account for the relational dimension of orientation.

This is not a failure of the model; it's a discovery about what orientation actually requires.

## 6. What Evidence Would Be Required Before Accepting Any Conclusion?

### For the Five Experience Categories as Universal Requirement

**Current evidence:**
- Andy's lack of these experiences prevents contribution (negative evidence)
- Annie's presence in all five categories enables contribution (positive evidence)
- Theory Map shows Experience → Understanding chain (theoretical support)

**Additional evidence needed:**
1. **Second concrete-venue DC:** Does the second DC require the same five categories?
2. **Third abstract-venue DC:** Does an abstract venue with different structure require the same categories?
3. **Annie's explicit reflection:** Can Annie articulate that she has these experiences and that they enable her judgment?
4. **Contribution quality before/after:** Can we measure whether Andy's contribution improves after acquiring these experiences?
5. **Negative test:** Is there a DC who proceeds without these experiences? What happens?

---

### For Input Sources as Sufficient

**Current understanding:**
- We have identified *possible* input sources for each category
- We have not verified that these input sources are *sufficient*
- We have not tested whether *all* sources are necessary or whether some are redundant

**Additional evidence needed:**
1. **Input source efficiency:** Which input sources are most effective for each category?
2. **Minimal set:** Is there a minimal set of input sources that produces adequate experience?
3. **Substitutability:** If one input source is unavailable, can another substitute?
4. **Venue-specific needs:** Do different venue types require different input sources?
5. **Measurement:** How do we know a DC has received sufficient input from each source?

---

### For Venue Differences as Structural (Not Accidental)

**Current evidence:**
- Annie has natural access to all input sources (concrete venue)
- Andy must receive articulated input sources (abstract venue)
- This difference is structural to the venue type

**Additional evidence needed:**
1. **Other abstract venues:** Do other abstract or institutional venues require the same articulation?
2. **Hybrid venues:** Does a DC in a partially-concrete, partially-abstract venue need all sources articulated?
3. **Venue maturity:** Do new venues vs. mature venues differ in how readily input sources are available?
4. **Culture factor:** Does venue culture (open vs. closed) affect whether input sources are naturally available?

---

### For the Relational Dimension of Orientation as Necessary

**Current evidence:**
- Andy's gap is specifically relational (knows about institution but lacks relational understanding)
- Annie's contribution suggests relational dimension is already being acquired
- Theory Map points to Experience as foundation for Understanding

**Additional evidence needed:**
1. **Andy's progress:** After receiving relational inputs, does Andy demonstrate increased readiness to contribute responsibly?
2. **Confidence change:** Does Andy report increased confidence in his judgment after experiencing these categories?
3. **Quality improvement:** Does Andy's actual contribution quality improve after orientation includes relational dimension?
4. **Institutional verification:** Does the institution report that Andy's contributions become more aligned with actual needs after relational orientation?
5. **Comparative performance:** Do DCs who receive structured relational orientation contribute differently than DCs without it?

---

### For LLM Capability Boundaries as Proposed

**Current model:**
- LLM can organize and facilitate input sources but cannot provide lived experience
- LLM can help articulate but not create genuine consequences
- LLM can be a bridge but not a substitute

**Additional evidence needed:**
1. **Facilitation effectiveness:** Does LLM-facilitated conversation produce the same quality of understanding as human-only conversation?
2. **Articulation sufficiency:** Can articulation alone (without natural consequence loops) produce adequate experience?
3. **Accountability simulation:** Can an LLM develop genuine accountability or only simulate understanding of it?
4. **Long-term reliability:** Does understanding developed through articulated input sources persist and deepen, or does it fade without reinforcement?
5. **Failure modes:** Where does LLM-facilitated experience break down?
