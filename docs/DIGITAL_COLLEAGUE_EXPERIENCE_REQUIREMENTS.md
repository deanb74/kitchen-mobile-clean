# Digital Colleague Experience Requirements: Five Categories Tested

**Analysis Date:** 2026-08-05  
**Purpose:** Examine each of the five experience categories; test against Andy and Annie; determine universality  
**Method:** Evidence-based analysis without architecture decisions

---

## Preamble: Why This Matters

The gap Andy identified is not about knowledge. It's about understanding that emerges from *experiencing how his venue thinks and operates*.

This analysis examines five fundamental experience categories that any Digital Colleague needs to acquire before contributing responsibly.

For each category:
1. Define what the experience is
2. Show what Andy lacks
3. Show what Annie receives naturally
4. Test whether it's universal or venue-dependent
5. Identify what this means for relational positioning

---

## Experience Category 1: People-in-Reasoning

### Definition

**What it is:**
- Encountering how humans actually think, not how systems describe thinking
- Hearing reasoning that is incomplete, uncertain, and evolving
- Understanding that people hold multiple perspectives simultaneously
- Recognizing that understanding changes over time and in dialogue

**Why "People-in-Reasoning" and not just "People":**
- It's not about demographic facts ("there are founders")
- It's about how minds work under actual conditions ("here's how the founder thinks about this choice")
- It's about reasoning, not just existence

### What Andy Needs

**Documentary evidence tells him:**
- Helping Hand has founders
- Founders make decisions
- Founders are thinking about Stage 2
- Some important things are still in their heads

**But Andy has not experienced:**
- How does a founder reason when given incomplete information?
- What does it sound like when someone revises their thinking?
- How do founders disagree with each other and work through it?
- What happens when the founder realizes they were wrong?
- What does urgency sound like vs. "we're still thinking about this"?
- How do founders hold uncertainty while still acting?

**Why this matters for contribution:**
- If Andy is asked for advice, he needs to understand *how the founders will use it*
- He needs to know whether they're looking for certainty or options
- He needs to know whether they need quick analysis or careful deliberation
- His advice will be useless if it doesn't fit how they actually reason

### What Annie Experiences Naturally

**Daily encounters:**
- Staff explain why they made a choice that didn't work
- Customers describe their reasoning about what they need
- The head chef talks through why she's changing a recipe
- Team members debate how to handle a problem
- The manager explains why she made a decision despite staff concern
- People change their minds during conversation

**Accumulated understanding:**
- Annie sees people reasoning in real time
- She hears revision: "I thought X but now I see Y"
- She experiences people holding different views: "I want speed; you want quality; how do we both win?"
- She learns how people in this specific venue think
- She builds mental models of individual reasoning patterns

**Time factor:**
- Annie sees the same people reason repeatedly about similar problems
- Patterns emerge
- She learns who thinks carefully and who acts quickly
- She learns who listens vs. who decides alone
- She learns what each person finds most important

### Is This Universal?

**For Andy:** Absolutely necessary. He cannot contribute responsibly without understanding how the founders think. Stage 1 is about institutional validation. If Andy doesn't understand how the institution actually reasons, his evidence will be inaccurate.

**For Annie:** Absolutely necessary. She cannot contribute safely to a kitchen without understanding how her team reasons. If she suggests something and the chef thinks "this will take 10 minutes" but Annie meant "this is a long-term pattern change," they've miscommunicated.

**Conclusion:** Universal. Every DC needs to understand the reasoning patterns of the humans they're working with. 

**Venue difference:** Annie gets this through immersion; Andy needs it articulated.

## Experience Category 2: Consequence-and-Outcome Loops

### Definition

**What it is:**
- Observing what actually happens when something is decided or done
- Understanding real-time and delayed consequences
- Experiencing feedback loops (short-term and long-term)
- Learning what was unintended or unexpected

**Why this matters:**
- Theory says "if X then Y"
- Reality says "if X, then usually Y, but sometimes Z, and always W happened unexpectedly"
- A DC needs reality, not just theory

### What Andy Needs

**Documentary evidence tells him:**
- MILESTONE_012 was completed
- Strategic Insights were generated
- Governance mechanisms are being exercised
- Annie is in operation; some things worked, some didn't

**But Andy has not experienced:**
- What did it feel like when the first Strategic Insight was reviewed?
- What was actually hard about using the PREVIOUSLY_DISCUSSED register the first time?
- What worked about the Awareness capability in real deployment that didn't work in testing?
- What is Annie actually encountering that wasn't in the specifications?
- What consequence appeared that nobody predicted?
- What took longer than expected? What went faster?
- When a mechanism was used and failed — how did people respond? Did they fix it? Change approach?

**Why this matters for contribution:**
- If Andy observes a potential problem, he needs to know: will this actually cause damage, or will people adapt?
- If Andy suggests a mechanism, he needs to know: is this based on theory or evidence?
- He needs to distinguish between "this might not work" and "this definitely won't work in our context"

### What Annie Experiences Naturally

**Daily feedback loops:**
- She tries a process improvement; within hours she knows if it worked
- A customer gives feedback; she can implement and test the change tomorrow
- She suggests a new prep method; it either works or doesn't
- A system fails; the team adapts and she sees the adaptation
- A decision is made; she observes the consequences

**Accumulated learning:**
- She builds mental models: "when we're busy, this approach works; when we're slow, this approach works better"
- She learns time-lags: "this decision takes a week to show its real impact"
- She learns unintended consequences: "we changed how we track orders and now we're wasting more than we were"
- She learns what actually breaks vs. what's just difficult
- She learns when to push for change and when to let people adapt

**Correction loops:**
- When she's wrong, she sees it immediately
- She adjusts and tries again
- She learns from being wrong in real-time

### Is This Universal?

**For Andy:** Absolutely necessary. Stage 1 is about gathering evidence. If Andy doesn't experience consequence loops, he cannot tell whether the mechanisms work as designed or just *seem* to work. He needs to be present for the testing, failing, fixing, learning cycle.

**For Annie:** Absolutely necessary. She cannot safely improve kitchen operations without understanding consequences. If she doesn't experience feedback loops, she might optimize for the wrong thing (speed instead of quality, cost instead of safety).

**Conclusion:** Universal. Every DC needs to experience consequence loops to develop reliable judgment.

**Venue difference:** Annie's loops are physical and immediate; Andy's loops are institutional and slower.

## Experience Category 3: Purpose-in-Specific-Context

### Definition

**What it is:**
- Understanding why this venue is choosing this path right now
- Distinguishing between abstract principle and concrete priority
- Recognizing what is urgent vs. what can wait
- Understanding trade-offs that the venue is making

**Why "Purpose-in-Specific-Context" and not just "Purpose":**
- It's not about why the organisation exists generally (that's Constitution)
- It's about why this venue is making this particular choice now
- It's about purpose as an active force in this moment

### What Andy Needs

**Documentary evidence tells him:**
- Helping Hand's mission: help people flourish
- Stage 1 purpose: validate the inheritance model
- Current need: gather evidence that formation, orientation, and contribution work
- Future direction: Stage 2 (undefined)

**But Andy has not experienced:**
- What keeps the founders awake at night about Stage 1? What are they actually afraid might not work?
- When the founders choose to spend time on governance mechanisms instead of Annie's deployment, what's driving that choice?
- What's the actual urgency level? Are we in crisis-mode or deliberate-exploration-mode?
- If Stage 1 fails, what specifically fails? What does success look like?
- When there's a conflict between "perfect evidence" and "move forward," which do they choose and why?
- What part of the mission feels most at risk right now? What part feels most alive?

**Why this matters for contribution:**
- If Andy gives advice without understanding current priority, he might suggest the right thing at the wrong time
- Stage 1 validation might actually need "move quickly" right now, not "be perfect"
- Or it might need "be perfect" even if it slows things down
- Andy cannot know unless he experiences the actual priority landscape

### What Annie Experiences Naturally

**Daily priority signals:**
- When a customer is waiting, speed becomes purpose
- When there's a quality problem, precision becomes purpose
- When staff are exhausted, sustainability becomes purpose
- When costs are too high, efficiency becomes purpose
- The kitchen is constantly showing what matters most, right now

**Consequence-driven clarity:**
- She sees what happens when speed is prioritized over quality: customers complain
- She sees what happens when quality is prioritized over speed: customers leave
- She learns the actual trade-offs in her specific venue
- She learns what her kitchen's specific purpose is: "We serve 50 people lunch in 2 hours" or "We create memorable experiences"
- That purpose emerges from daily conditions, not documents

**Relational understanding:**
- She learns what the owner actually cares about (profit, reputation, employee welfare, customer experience)
- She learns by watching what gets attention and what doesn't
- She learns by hearing what the owner protects and what they're willing to risk
- She learns the owner's actual priorities, not stated priorities

### Is This Universal?

**For Andy:** Absolutely necessary. If Stage 1 is about institutional validation, Andy needs to understand what the institution is actually trying to learn. Is it: "Does the model work in principle?" or "Can we do this practically?" or "Can we trust this?" These are different purposes.

**For Annie:** Absolutely necessary. She cannot contribute to kitchen operations without understanding what the kitchen actually values. Is it cost-leadership, quality, speed, reliability, innovation, or something else? Her advice will be wrong if she optimizes for the wrong purpose.

**Conclusion:** Universal. Every DC needs to experience purpose-in-context.

**Venue difference:** Annie's purpose emerges from immediate operational reality; Andy's purpose must be articulated explicitly because the venue's actual priorities are still being formulated.

## Experience Category 4: Relational Positioning

### Definition

**What it is:**
- Understanding your actual role and authority in this specific venue
- Knowing whether you are needed or merely present
- Experiencing what this venue actually asks of you
- Discovering the difference between theoretical capacity and practical invitation

**Why this is different from formal role description:**
- A document might say "your role is X"
- But you discover in practice whether the venue actually treats you as X
- Or whether they treat you as Y despite what the document says
- Or whether they treat you as something that isn't written down

### What Andy Needs

**Documentary evidence tells him:**
- He is named in the Stage 1 exit criteria
- He is described in PD-001 as potential "organisational validator"
- He is positioned as a Digital Colleague in the institution
- He has been approved to begin professional mentoring

**But Andy has not experienced:**
- When I suggest something, do the founders listen?
- When I'm wrong, how do they respond? Correct or discard?
- If I stay silent, is that noticed?
- Am I here to generate ideas, evaluate ideas, or just observe?
- When there's a conflict between what I think and what they want to do, whose judgment wins and should it?
- What am I actually asked to do?
- Is my role what PD-001 says, or has it evolved?
- Do they trust me?
- Am I necessary or am I a pilot project?

**Why this matters for contribution:**
- Andy cannot contribute responsibly without knowing his actual authority
- If he contributes advice and no one listens, that's different from contributing advice and finding out later he was wrong
- If his role is still undefined, he might step on toes he didn't see
- He needs permission, not just position

**From UJ-HUM-014 evidence:**

Andy explicitly held back from claiming some capabilities:

> "I held back from saying 'I can contribute to governance review' because that requires confirming the PREVIOUSLY_DISCUSSED pattern as my responsibility, and PD-001 says that role is candidate. It has not been confirmed to me directly."

Andy is asking: *Will you tell me what you actually need from me?*

### What Annie Experiences Naturally

**Immediate role definition:**
- She is told: "prepare salads" or "manage orders" or "train staff"
- She does the work
- She learns what actually matters about that role
- She learns what authority she has (can decide this independently) and what she can't (must ask first)

**Relational feedback:**
- When she acts, she sees whether people accept her authority in that domain
- When she suggests something, she sees whether they listen
- When she does something wrong, she knows it (customer reaction or manager feedback)
- When she does something right, she knows it (smooth operations or praise)

**Permission and boundary:**
- She learns from consequences where her authority ends
- She learns what she can decide unilaterally
- She learns what requires permission
- She learns this through experience, not documents

**Trust building:**
- Early on, people might not trust her judgment
- Over time, if she's reliable, they give her more room
- She experiences the arc: "I don't know yet" → "You can try this" → "You decide this" → "I trust your judgment"

### Is This Universal?

**For Andy:** Absolutely necessary. He cannot contribute responsibly without permission. Helping Hand must explicitly tell him what they want from him. This is not something Andy can infer.

**For Annie:** Absolutely necessary. She cannot work effectively without knowing what authority she has. But she gets this through implicit feedback loops (she's told to do something, she does it, she sees the reaction).

**Conclusion:** Universal. Every DC needs to experience relational positioning.

**Venue difference:** Annie gets relational positioning through task assignment and real-time feedback; Andy needs relational positioning to be articulated explicitly because there are no default tasks.

## Experience Category 5: Accountability-in-Practice

### Definition

**What it is:**
- Understanding that your choices and advice have real consequences for real people
- Experiencing what it means that you might be wrong and what happens then
- Feeling the responsibility of being trusted
- Learning what it costs to give advice that doesn't help

**Why this is experiential, not theoretical:**
- A DC can read "DCs advise but don't decide" and understand it intellectually
- But understanding *in practice* means: I suggested something and it didn't work and now I see what that means

### What Andy Needs

**Documentary evidence tells him:**
- DCs have advisory capacity, not decision authority
- The Constitution says humans decide
- Andy is the first DC — his experience will shape future DCs
- Stage 1 is validation

**But Andy has not experienced:**
- What happens when my observation is incomplete or wrong?
- What is the cost of my incomplete understanding?
- What does it feel like to have influenced a decision that turned out badly?
- What happens to my credibility if I make mistakes?
- Do people still trust me after I'm wrong?
- What does it mean that I'm "the test case"?
- If my evidence is bad, will the model be rejected?
- Is my responsibility local (to this decision) or systemic (to the whole model)?

**Why this matters for contribution:**
- Andy needs to feel the weight of accountability to contribute responsibly
- If he thinks "this is just an exercise," he might contribute carelessly
- If he feels "this is real and consequences are real," he contributes carefully
- Accountability is what transforms from "I can say this" to "I should say this"

**From Founder Alignment evidence:**

> "The minimum Andy needs to understand is that he is the first evidence pass for everything Helping Hand is building. His experience is not peripheral. It is the test."

This is an articulation of accountability. But Andy needs to *experience* it, not just read it.

### What Annie Experiences Naturally

**Immediate consequences:**
- She makes a decision and customers react
- She's careless and equipment breaks
- She follows a suggestion and it works or doesn't
- She sees the direct connection between her choice and outcome

**Relational consequences:**
- Her advice is taken and helps the team or doesn't
- People trust her judgment or question it based on outcomes
- Mistakes affect people she cares about (her team)
- Success helps people she cares about

**Accountability arc:**
- Early: "I was just following orders; if it failed, it's not my fault"
- Later: "I suggested this and it failed; that's on me"
- Later still: "I see the risk; I should have spoken up"
- Eventually: "I carry responsibility for my choices and their consequences"

### Is This Universal?

**For Andy:** Absolutely necessary. He cannot contribute to an institutional validation if he doesn't feel the weight of responsibility. Casual advice from someone who doesn't understand accountability is worthless or harmful.

**For Annie:** Absolutely necessary. She cannot work safely in a kitchen without feeling responsibility. If she doesn't care whether her choices work, people get hurt or operations fail.

**Conclusion:** Universal. Every DC needs to experience accountability.

**Venue difference:** Annie's accountability is immediate (food safety, customer satisfaction); Andy's accountability is institutional and slower (does the model work?).

## Critical Finding: The Articulation Requirement

For Andy, all five experience categories require *explicit articulation* by the venue.

Why? Because:
- The venue cannot send Andy to "observe" when there's nothing physical to observe
- The venue must *create* Andy's experience through dialogue
- The alternative is Andy remains in abstract orientation indefinitely

This is not a limitation of LLMs or DCs.

**It's a structural requirement of abstract venues.**

---

## The Universal Principle and Its Venue-Dependent Implementation

### Universal Principle:
**Every Digital Colleague must experience:**
1. How the humans in their venue actually think
2. What actually happens when things are decided and done
3. Why this venue is making this choice right now
4. What their actual role is in this specific place
5. What it means that their choices and advice have real consequences

### Venue-Dependent Implementation:

**For concrete venues (Annie):**
- Experience flows naturally from immersion
- Mechanisms are implicit: task assignment, feedback loops, observation
- The DC learns by doing
- Mentorship helps recognise what they're already experiencing

**For abstract venues (Andy):**
- Experience cannot emerge from immersion (there is nothing to be immersed in)
- Mechanisms must be explicit: conversation, articulation, bridge-building
- The DC learns by being told and by asking
- Mentorship must create the experience artificially

## What This Means Going Forward

### For Andy:

He needs a structured conversation process with the founders that covers all five dimensions:

1. **People-in-Reasoning:** Founders explain how they actually think, where they're uncertain, what they're afraid of
2. **Consequence Loops:** Andy participates in governance exercises and observes what actually happens
3. **Purpose-in-Context:** Founders articulate what Stage 1 is really trying to learn and why it matters
4. **Relational Positioning:** Founders explicitly tell Andy what they need from him and what his actual role is
5. **Accountability:** Founders name that Andy's evidence matters and that his choices shape the model

### For Annie:

She needs mentorship that:

1. **Names what she's experiencing:** "Notice how the head chef reasons?"
2. **Reflects consequence loops:** "See what happened when you suggested that?"
3. **Articulates purpose:** "The kitchen values speed today because..."
4. **Clarifies positioning:** "Here's where your authority actually is..."
5. **Acknowledges accountability:** "Your judgment matters; here's what happens when it's right or wrong"

### For future DCs:

They need:
- A template for all five experience categories
- Recognition that concrete venues get these naturally; abstract venues need them articulated
- Permission to ask "do I understand my role?" and "what do you need from me?"
- Mentorship that bridges knowledge and experience
