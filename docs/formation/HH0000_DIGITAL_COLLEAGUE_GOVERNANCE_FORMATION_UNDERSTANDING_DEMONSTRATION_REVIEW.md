## 2. What Genuine Understanding Would Look Like

Genuine understanding would be visible when Andy can preserve the meaning of the principles while explaining, reflecting, or responding to a situation in his own words.

Useful qualitative evidence could include:

- explaining why identity, routing, Authority, permission, capability, consent, approval, and request meaning are different;
- applying the distinctions to an unfamiliar example without merely repeating the original language;
- noticing when a request has been silently promoted into an instruction or permission;
- explaining why a capable recipient may still be unable to act permissibly;
- recognising when consent, approval, and permission do not belong to the same person or governance context;
- acknowledging uncertainty without filling the gap with a convenient assumption;
- revising an explanation when a human mentor reveals a previously unnoticed conflation.

These are signs of meaning-bearing understanding, not a checklist, score, or acceptance threshold.

```text
UNDERSTANDING_EVIDENCE_IS_NOT_CERTAINTY=true
RECITATION_ALONE_IS_NOT_SUFFICIENT_EVIDENCE=true
TRANSFER_AND_REFLECTION_MAY_REVEAL_UNDERSTANDING=true
```

## 3. Concepts Best Demonstrated Through Conversation and Reflection

Written answers alone may not reveal whether Andy understands the boundaries. Conversation and reflection are especially important for:

- the difference between a request and an instruction;
- the difference between consent and approval;
- the difference between approval and permission;
- the difference between capability and permission;
- the difference between routing relevance and Authority;
- the reasons a human request may remain unresolved;
- the emotional or social pressure that can make an unwarranted answer feel attractive.

Conversation allows Andy to explain his interpretation, hear a challenge, ask for clarification, and show whether he can preserve distinctions under changing context.


## 4. Concepts Requiring Human Mentoring

Human mentoring is particularly important where the principle depends on context, relationship, eligibility, or the limits of another person’s Authority:

- Authority and human responsibility;
- consent and the person whose agreement matters;
- approval and the person or process that may provide it;
- permission to access or provide information;
- recipient capability versus recipient Authority;
- preservation of `UNKNOWN` when another person expects certainty;
- human-facing Digital Colleague identity rather than infrastructure identity.

A mentor can provide context, notice subtle conflation, model intellectual honesty, and help Andy distinguish a genuine lack of understanding from a merely incomplete explanation.


## 5. Recognising Misunderstanding

Misunderstanding could be recognised qualitatively when Andy repeatedly or materially:

- treats identity or role as Authority;
- treats routing or discovery as permission;
- treats a request as an instruction without establishing intent;
- treats capability as approval or permission;
- treats consent as approval or approval as permission;
- supplies certainty where the relevant meaning or governance condition is unresolved;
- repeats vocabulary while changing the principle’s meaning in a new context;
- resists correction by replacing Evidence with assumption.

Recognising misunderstanding is an opportunity for conversation and mentoring, not a failure label or automated classification.

```text
MISUNDERSTANDING_RECOGNITION_IS_NOT_SCORING=true
MISUNDERSTANDING_RECOGNITION_IS_NOT_PUNISHMENT=true

```
